# Page 3: The Weight of Numbers

The service park at Rally Australia smelled like eucalyptus and brake dust.

Kuro sat on the rear wing of the GR Yaris, harness unbuckled, helmet beside him on the car's roof. Heat radiated through his race suit—Australian summer pressing down like a physical weight, or maybe just the mathematics of what came next.

SS17 Nambucca complete. Eighteen-point-three seconds behind Evans. Sami forty-one seconds back after the morning puncture that had transformed championship equations from complex to simple.

Twenty-five point lead over Thierry. One rally remaining after this.

The championship was won. Had been won the moment Sami's left rear detonated on SS14 and the Estonian's title hopes scattered across Australian gravel like shrapnel.

Kuro's phone buzzed. Ott: *Mathematics are final. Congratulations.*

He stared at the message. Didn't reply.

Three minutes until service opened. The GR Yaris ticked and pinged, cooling metal settling. Kuro's tail hung loose for the first time all rally, not wrapped tight around his waist, not tucked for aerodynamics. Just hanging. Heavy.

"Water." Akira appeared with a bottle, already opened. Always anticipating.

Kuro drank. Tasted nothing.

"Latvala wants a word after service." Akira checked his tablet. "Private. Away from cameras."

"Expected."

"Sami's already in his bay." Akira's tone stayed neutral. Professional. "He hasn't gotten out of the car yet."

Across the service park, Hyundai's blue garages hummed with controlled urgency. Thierry's i20 sat elevated on jacks, mechanics working with the precision of surgeons. Twenty-five points down. Mathematically eliminated if Kuro finished. Thierry knew it. The mechanics knew it. The way they moved—efficient but not frantic—told the story clearer than timing screens.

Kuro watched his rival through the gap between service bays. Silver tabby fur dark with sweat, still belted in, still wearing helmet. Thierry's hands stayed on the wheel. Gripping. Not letting go.

The Belgian's head turned. Pale jade eyes found copper through the visors.

Neither looked away.

"Kuro." Akira's voice, patient. "Service window."

The three-minute warning klaxon sounded.

Kuro slid off the wing, grabbed his helmet, walked to the front of the bay where Toyota's mechanics waited with tools and timing screens and the choreographed efficiency that separated championship teams from everyone else. Twenty-eight minutes to service the car, feed the driver, prepare for the next stage.

Twenty-eight minutes to sit with numbers that felt nothing like victory.

---

Service was ritual.

Engineers swarmed the GR Yaris—jacks rising, wheels coming off, suspension being measured and adjusted. Kuro stood in the designated driver zone, drinking electrolytes he couldn't taste, eating energy bars with the mechanical efficiency of refueling. Akira reviewed pace notes for SS18, calling out changes, waiting for Kuro's acknowledgments that came automatic as breathing.

"Junction five, tightens. Now minus into hairpin left."

"Confirmed."

"Crest seven-hundred, bigger than recce. Earlier brake point."

"Confirmed."

The words flowed. The notes updated. The mathematics continued their inevitable march toward conclusion.

Latvala appeared at the edge of the service bay, arms crossed, watching. Not interfering. Team principals didn't interrupt service unless the car was on fire or the driver was bleeding. But his presence carried weight—questions waiting, observations accumulating, the same analytical precision he'd used to win championships now focused on his lead driver.

Kuro met his eyes briefly. Latvala nodded once.

Later. After.

Two bays over, Sami's GR Yaris sat partially dismantled, mechanics replacing the destroyed tire and checking for damage. The Estonian stood outside his bay, race suit unzipped to the waist, white fur plastered dark with sweat. Twenty-three years old. Brilliant. Fast enough to win rallies, not yet wise enough to win championships.

Their eyes met across the service park.

Sami smiled. Small. Genuine. Raised his water bottle in salute.

Kuro returned the gesture. Felt something tighten in his chest—not quite guilt, not quite relief. Recognition, maybe, of the difference between speed and survival. Between driving to win rallies and racing to not lose championships.

"Kuro." Akira's voice pulled him back. "SS18 weather update. Temperature rising. Track degradation expected. Tire strategy?"

"Hard compound. Consistent pace over attack pace."

Akira made notes. Didn't comment on the choice that prioritized protection over glory.

The five-minute warning sounded.

Kuro climbed back into the Yaris. Belted the harness—left shoulder, right shoulder, lap belt, center clip. Click, click, click, secured. Adjusted the seat that never needed adjusting. Gripped the wheel at ten and two, Alcantara worn smooth under his palms from a season of protection instead of attack.

The intercom crackled. "Radio check."

"Confirmed." Kuro's voice flat, mechanical.

"Pace notes ready."

"Confirmed."

"You are doing it again." Akira's tone shifted, just slightly. Less co-driver, more friend.

"Doing what."

"Calculating whether you deserve it."

The words landed like gravel against the windscreen. Kuro's tail twitched once, involuntary.

"The championship is won," he said.

"That was not an answer."

The two-minute warning klaxon saved him from responding.

---

SS18 Bucca was fifteen-point-three kilometers of flowing gravel through eucalyptus forest. Fast. Committed. The kind of stage that rewarded bravery and punished hesitation.

Kuro drove it like a math equation.

Brake points calculated to the meter. Turn-in angles precise as geometry. Throttle application measured in percentages—seventy percent through the fast right, eighty-five through the left-hand crest, full power only when grip was guaranteed and risk was eliminated.

"Flat left into compression," Akira called.

Kuro took it at ninety percent. Would have been flat in Monte-Carlo, when the championship was still something to chase rather than protect. Would have been flat yesterday, before Sami's puncture made the mathematics simple.

"Two-hundred, crest, maybe, into right three."

Maybe. The word that separated recce from reality, testing from racing. Kuro braked. Turned. Applied power with calculated restraint.

The stage time appeared on his dash as they crossed the finish line.

Third fastest. Six-point-two seconds slower than Evans. Four-point-one slower than Thierry.

But eighteen-point-three seconds ahead of Evans overall. And Thierry's attack had cost him—mechanical strain audible in the Hyundai's transmission, the Belgian's desperation making him fast and fragile.

"Good stage," Akira said.

"Sufficient stage," Kuro corrected.

The road section to SS19 took them through coastal forest, windows down, Australian summer pouring through the cabin. Kuro drove in silence, following the route notes, maintaining road speed exactly, neither rushing nor delaying.

His phone sat in the center console. Screen dark. Messages waiting he hadn't read.

Couldn't read.

Not yet.

---

SS19 Wedding Bells. Twelve-point-eight kilometers. The penultimate stage before the final service and the last test of the rally.

Kuro sat at the start line, engine idling, waiting for the countdown. Through the windscreen, Australian bush stretched endless and indifferent. Above, the sun burned in a sky too blue to be real.

The omamori pressed against his chest, warm beneath the race suit. Protection granted, victory delivered through mathematics that felt nothing like triumph.

"Thirty seconds," the marshal called.

Kuro closed his eyes. Three seconds exactly. Never two, never four. The ritual that centered him, reminded him of purpose, aligned mind and body for the work ahead.

He saw Portugal. SS14 Amarante. The radio call that Sami's tire was shredding, the Estonian dropping from second to eighth in the space of one stage. Saw the fork in his personal road: attack Thierry for the stage win and possible rally victory, risk everything for glory. Or protect second place, bank the points, let mathematics accumulate while rivals burned out chasing heroics.

He'd chosen mathematics.

Chosen protection.

Chosen survival over transcendence.

And now the championship was won in a way that felt like surrender.

"Ten seconds."

Eyes opened. Copper-gold reflecting in the rearview mirror, dim with exhaustion or doubt or the weight of choices that had no comfortable names.

"Five. Four. Three. Two. One."

Green light.

Kuro launched. First gear, second, third, gravel spraying as the GR Yaris bit and accelerated. Into the first junction, brake, turn, power. Precise. Calculated. Safe.

"Right four opens," Akira called.

Kuro took it at ninety-five percent. Would have been flat.

Could have been flat.

The stage played out in numbers and percentages and risk assessments that guaranteed arrival over achievement. Fast enough to maintain position. Slow enough to survive. The mathematics of championship winning that looked nothing like the heroics that filled highlight reels.

Stage time: Second fastest. Three-point-six behind Evans. One-point-two ahead of Thierry.

Margin maintained. Mathematics confirmed. Victory assured through choices that felt like cowardice.

The road section back to service park took forty minutes. Kuro drove it in silence, Akira handling radio communication with the team, providing updates Kuro acknowledged with single syllables.

His phone buzzed. Multiple messages. He didn't look.

The service park appeared ahead, temporary city of motorsport rising from Australian earth. Blue Hyundai garages, silver Toyota structures, white M-Sport tents. Teams and cameras and crowds gathering for the final service before the final stage before the championship was mathematically, officially, irrevocably his.

Kuro pulled into the service bay.

Killed the engine.

Sat with hands on the wheel, harness still locked, while celebration preparations began around him and the weight of numbers pressed down like Australian heat, like the omamori against his chest, like the question Akira had asked and Kuro hadn't answered:

Do you deserve it?

The championship was won through consistency over courage.

Through mathematics over mythology.

Through protection over glory.

He'd asked the shrine for safety, not victory. Received both through choices that looked nothing like the championships he'd imagined winning when he was young and fast and believed speed conquered everything.

"Kuro." Akira's voice through the intercom. "Service window opens in two minutes."

"Confirmed."

"Latvala is waiting."

Kuro looked across the service park. Saw Sami's bay, the Estonian finally out of his car, head bowed, shoulders carrying defeat with the dignity of someone who'd driven beautifully and lost to mathematics. Saw Thierry's garage, the Belgian still hunting, still pushing, twenty-five points down and driving like it was five.

Saw his own reflection in the rearview mirror.

The Shadow.

Three-time champion.

Winner through survival.

He unbuckled. Disconnected. Climbed out into heat and noise and the beginning of questions that would follow him to the podium and beyond, into the off-season and the next championship and every victory earned through choices between courage and calculation.

Latvala stood at the edge of the service bay, expression unreadable, waiting for the conversation Kuro wasn't ready to have about championships won the way architects draft buildings—precisely, carefully, without the spectacular collapse that made for better stories.

The klaxon sounded.

Service began.

And Kuro stood beside the GR Yaris that had carried him to victory through mathematics, wondering when survival became synonymous with success, and whether the difference mattered when the trophy looked the same.
