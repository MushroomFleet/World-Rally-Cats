# Page 0: The Third Prayer

The shrine stood where it had for two thousand years.

Kuro's rental car rolled to a stop in the gravel parking area. Engine ticked in the October heat. Beyond the torii gate, Ise Grand Shrine waited—wooden architecture that had been rebuilt every twenty years since before memory, continuity through deliberate impermanence.

He had not planned this.

Monte-Carlo was twelve weeks away. Pre-season testing started in seven. The flight from Nagoya to Tokyo for sponsor commitments left in four hours. This detour added ninety minutes to a schedule already compressed beyond rational margins.

He got out anyway.

The path was raked gravel, white stones that crunched beneath racing boots he'd worn straight from Toyota headquarters. Salary negotiations had run long. Contract extension through 2028. Number one car secured. Ogier was moving to partial schedule. The seat was his—two championships, seventeen wins, forty-seven podiums. The mathematics were unambiguous.

Akira had asked him afterward: "You look unsatisfied with outcome that gives you everything you wanted."

Kuro hadn't answered. Couldn't name the thing sitting in his chest like ballast.

The torii gate marked transition from mundane to sacred. He passed through. Copper eyes tracked architectural precision—posts and lintel joined without nails, wood fitted to wood through geometry and craft. The principles reminded him of racing lines. Optimal path through space, forces balanced, nothing wasted.

Families moved through the grounds. Kits chased each other near the temizuya, laughing while parents prepared ritual purification. An elderly calico couple walked slowly, tails entwined, probably married here six decades ago. A young tabby in business suit stood before the haiden, head bowed, praying for—what? Promotion? Love? Victory in something that mattered?

Kuro found the temizuya. Followed the ritual precisely: right paw to ladle, pour water over left paw. Left paw to ladle, pour over right paw. Right paw again, pour water into left paw, rinse mouth. Tilt ladle vertical to cleanse handle. Return ladle to rest.

The movements calmed him. Procedure. Sequence. Correct execution.

He approached the haiden. Dropped five-yen coin in offering box—the coin with a hole in its center, go-en, phonetically identical to "honorable relationship." Bowed twice. Clapped twice. The sound echoed.

Prayer.

He'd done this before races. Before championships. Before the career-defining moments when everything compressed to stage start and pace notes and commitment through sections where telemetry couldn't guide him.

Those prayers had worked. Two championships. Toyota contract. Number one car.

So why did standing here feel like standing at a crest approach, knowing he should stay flat through compression but calculating the safer line instead?

"Grant me victory in the upcoming season."

The words came automatically. First prayer. The one he'd spoken before 2023, before 2024, before every rally that built the reputation that made him The Shadow.

He waited.

The shrine was silent except for breeze through cryptomeria trees.

"Grant me safety through the stages ahead."

Second prayer. The practical one. Rally sport killed. Everyone knew the statistics. Everyone remembered the champions who'd pushed past the margins and paid the price. Safety wasn't weakness. Safety was survival mathematics.

The words felt correct.

The words felt hollow.

A kit's laughter carried from somewhere beyond the haiden. Pure joy, uncomplicated by calculation or consequence. Kuro's ears tracked the sound automatically, same way they tracked engine notes during stages, always listening for the warning that preceded failure.

His hand found the space over his heart.

What was the third prayer?

The question surfaced from somewhere he couldn't map. He stood before the haiden, two prayers delivered, coin offered, ritual observed. He should bow again. Should complete the sequence. Should return to schedule and sponsor commitments and the four-hour drive to Tokyo that was now three hours forty minutes because he'd stopped at a shrine seeking something he couldn't name.

Instead he stayed.

Around him, other visitors came and went. Prayed their prayers. Made their offerings. Moved through the sacred space with the confidence of people who knew what they were asking for.

Kuro didn't.

Victory—yes. Safety—obviously. But the third prayer hovered just beyond reach, like the perfect racing line he could see in telemetry but couldn't execute because execution required commitment his calculations declared irrational.

He closed his eyes.

The darkness behind his eyelids was absolute. Bombay cat black, the genetic gift that made him The Shadow. The darkness was comfortable. Familiar. Safe.

In the darkness, he could almost hear it.

The thing he'd felt at Monte-Carlo 2023, SS1, when championship points didn't exist yet and the season was blank canvas. He'd driven that stage committed. Full throttle through the uncertain sections. Trusted the notes. Trusted the car. Trusted—

He opened his eyes.

A priest moved through the grounds near the main shrine building. Ancient tortoiseshell in traditional white and blue robes. The priest's movements were deliberate, practiced, part of the ritual maintenance that kept sacred space sacred.

Kuro found himself walking toward him.

"Excuse me." The words came out quieter than intended.

The priest turned. Yellow eyes studied Kuro with the patience of someone who'd witnessed ten thousand prayers and understood that some visitors needed more than ritual.

"Yes, young one?"

"I am—" Kuro paused. Three seconds. Calculated introduction. "I am here to pray. For the coming season. I race rally cars. For Toyota."

"I know who you are." The priest's expression showed gentle amusement. "Two championships. The Shadow. My grandson watches every rally. He speaks of your consistency."

Consistency. The word should have felt like compliment. Instead it landed like diagnosis.

"I have made two prayers," Kuro said. "There should be three. I cannot remember what the third should be."

The priest nodded slowly. "And this troubles you."

"Yes."

"Because you are precise driver. Preparation and ritual. Everything calculated."

"Yes."

"But this third prayer—it resists calculation."

Kuro's tail wrapped tighter around his waist. The priest saw anyway. Decades of observing visitors had taught him to read the things people tried to hide.

"Come." The priest gestured toward a bench beneath a cryptomeria tree. "Sit. The shrine has stood for two thousand years. It will stand through one conversation."

They sat. Late afternoon sun filtered through evergreen branches. The grounds were quieter now, families departing before evening.

"Tell me about your two prayers," the priest said.

"Victory. Safety."

"And these are good prayers. Appropriate for someone in dangerous profession." The priest folded his paws. "But they are not complete."

"How do you know?"

"Because you are here. Because you stopped your schedule to visit shrine when you had not planned to. Because the third prayer is the one you cannot name—and unnamed prayers are the ones that matter most."

Kuro watched the branches above. Tried to find counterargument. Found only wind and leaves and the uncomfortable truth that he'd driven ninety minutes out of his way because contract extension felt less like victory than it should have.

"When I started racing," he said slowly, "I prayed for opportunity. For someone to see talent and give me chance. That prayer was answered. Toyota saw. Gave me seat. Gave me car. Gave me everything I calculated I would need to become champion."

"And you became champion."

"Twice."

"So the prayers worked."

"Yes."

"Then why does third prayer elude you?"

The question sat between them. Kuro's copper eyes tracked falling leaf, calculated trajectory, predicted landing point. Accuracy within three centimeters. The mathematics came automatically.

"Because I do not know what I am afraid of losing," he finally said.

The priest nodded. "Now we approach truth."

"Victory—I am afraid of not winning. Safety—I am afraid of injury or death. These fears are logical. Addressable through prayer and preparation." Kuro's claws extended slightly, pressed against his palms. "But third prayer. Third fear. I cannot calculate its shape."

"Perhaps because it is not fear of losing something external. Perhaps it is fear of losing something within yourself."

The words landed precisely. Surgical strike against defenses built from consistent results and championship mathematics.

Kuro's breathing slowed. Technique he used before stage starts. Calm the system. Reduce variables. Execute sequence.

"At Monte-Carlo SS1, two seasons ago," he said, "I drove committed. Full throttle. Trusted the notes completely. Finished second on stage but felt—" He paused. Searched for word. "Victorious. Despite second place. The feeling was more real than championship trophy three months later."

"And now?"

"Now I drive calculated. Protect points. Minimize risk. Win championships through consistency that feels—" Another pause. Longer this time. "Empty."

The priest said nothing. Let silence work.

"If I pray for victory and safety but lose the thing that made victory meaningful," Kuro continued, "what have I protected? What have I won?"

"Perhaps that is your third prayer."

Kuro looked at him. "To not lose something I cannot name?"

"To remember what you are racing toward instead of what you are racing to avoid." The priest stood. Brushed needles from his robes. "Victory and safety are good prayers. But they are prayers of protection. Third prayer should be prayer of purpose. What did you love before you learned to be afraid of losing it?"

The question followed Kuro back to the haiden.

He stood before the offering box again. The five-yen coin was long gone, mixed with ten thousand other prayers. He dropped another coin. Bowed twice. Clapped twice.

"Grant me victory in the upcoming season."

First prayer. Unchanged.

"Grant me safety through the stages ahead."

Second prayer. Required.

His mouth opened for the third prayer.

The words wouldn't come.

He stood there. Hands together. Eyes closed. The third prayer hovering just beyond reach like the perfect racing line he could see but couldn't drive because driving it required trusting something calculation couldn't measure.

What did he love before he learned to be afraid?

Monte-Carlo SS1. The committed line. Full throttle through uncertainty. The feeling of dancing with car and stage and co-driver, all of them synchronized in movement that transcended mathematics.

He'd felt alive.

When had alive become dangerous?

When had protection become the goal instead of the price?

Kuro's eyes opened. The prayer remained unspoken. The third prayer that was supposed to complete the sequence, that would protect him through the coming season, that would keep him safe and victorious and—

Empty.

He bowed again. Turned from the haiden. Walked back through the grounds.

The priest was gone. The families were gone. Late afternoon had become early evening. His schedule was destroyed. Tokyo sponsor commitments would have to be rescheduled. Akira would ask what happened. Toyota would want explanations for the delay.

He had no explanation that survived calculation.

The rental car started precisely. Fuel gauge showed adequate reserves for Tokyo. Navigation system displayed optimal route. Everything calculated, everything planned, everything protected.

Kuro pulled out of the parking area. The torii gate receded in the mirror—sacred space becoming mundane space, transition marked by wooden architecture that had stood for two thousand years through deliberate impermanence.

His phone showed seven missed calls. Three from Akira. Two from Toyota PR. Two from his sister Yuki.

He didn't call back.

Couldn't explain that he'd stopped at Ise Grand Shrine to pray for the coming season and left with two prayers spoken and one prayer unnamed and the growing certainty that unnamed prayer was the one that mattered most.

The highway stretched ahead. Tokyo lights appeared on the horizon. Monte-Carlo was twelve weeks away. Pre-season testing in seven. Contract extension through 2028 secured. Number one car confirmed. Everything aligned for third championship through the same consistency mathematics that had delivered two previous titles.

The mathematics were elegant.

The mathematics were suffocating.

Kuro drove in silence, copper eyes tracking optimal racing line through evening traffic, calculating everything except the answer to the question the priest had left him:

What did he love before he learned to be afraid of losing it?

The answer followed him to Tokyo. Through sponsor commitments delivered with hollow precision. Through pre-season testing where telemetry showed he was still fast enough but something felt calibrated instead of committed. Through Monte-Carlo where he drove to second place overall through seventeen stages of protected mathematics.

Through Sweden. Portugal. Kenya. Croatia.

The unnamed third prayer followed him through every stage, every rally, every championship point that accumulated toward the title he would win in Poland through mathematics that felt less like victory with each passing kilometer.

Twenty-five point lead over Thierry. Sami forty-one back. Two rallies remaining.

The prayer he couldn't speak at Ise Grand Shrine became the weight he carried into Warsaw hotel room at 02:17, studying telemetry from SS14 Mikołajki, watching his red racing line diverge from Evans' blue optimal line at the fast left crest.

Ninety percent throttle. Safe compression. Second fastest time.

Protected mathematics.

Unnamed prayer.

Third championship approaching through sequence he'd perfected until perfection felt like prison.

Australia waited. Latvala wanted private meeting. Recce notes needed preparation. The championship required protection.

But the third prayer—the one he'd left unspoken at Ise Grand Shrine eight months ago—still waited for the answer he was afraid to give:

What happens when you stop protecting what you've won and remember why you wanted to win it?

The question had no calculation.

The question had no safe answer.

The question followed him from shrine to Warsaw to Australia where something had to change or the third championship would taste exactly like Poland SS14.

Sufficient. Safe. Hollow.

Not victory.

Not anymore.

Not unless he remembered the third prayer.

Not unless he found the courage to speak it.
