# Page 2: The Mathematics of Protection

The flight from Poland landed at Sydney International at 04:47 local time.

Kuro hadn't slept. Didn't try. Sat in business class with stage notes for Rally Australia spread across the tray table, studying junction angles and crest compressions with the same methodical focus he'd applied since Rally Poland ended three days ago.

Twenty-five point lead over Thierry. Sami forty-one points back. Two rallies remaining.

The numbers that should have felt like victory.

Across the aisle, Akira slept with his tablet still displaying SS14 Mikołajki telemetry—the stage where Kuro had lifted two-tenths before the fast left-hand crest. Where Evans had stayed flat and gained three-point-six seconds. Where the championship lead had grown from twenty-two points to twenty-five through arithmetic that rewarded survival over speed.

Kuro's phone sat dark on the tray table beside the notes. Messages from Yuki waited, unread. His sister's congratulations for Poland, her questions about Australia, her observations he wasn't ready to parse.

The seatbelt light pinged on. Descent beginning.

He gathered the notes. Precise stack, edges aligned. Slid them into the leather portfolio Toyota had given him after the 2024 championship—embossed with his name, weight optimized, corners reinforced. Everything calculated. Everything protected.

Through the window, Australian coastline emerged from pre-dawn darkness. Unfamiliar continent. Unknown roads. The final test before Japan, before the championship resolved into permanence.

"You reviewed those seven times." Akira's voice, sleep-rough but alert. Always alert. "The notes have not changed since Warsaw."

"Consistency requires repetition."

"So does obsession."

Kuro didn't respond. The aircraft banked, Sydney sprawling beneath them—harbor lights reflecting off water, city grid spreading inland with geometric precision. His tail twitched once against the seat, involuntary recognition of the pattern below.

Latvala waited at arrivals with the Toyota Australia team liaison. No cameras. No media circus. Deliberate timing—arrive before the press, before the spectators, before the service park opened for scrutiny.

"Kuro. Akira." Latvala's handshake was firm, eyes searching. Team principal assessing his lead driver the way engineers read telemetry. "Flight acceptable?"

"Sufficient."

"Recce starts at ten hundred. Media obligations at fourteen hundred." Latvala gestured toward the waiting SUV. "But first, we talk. Private."

The hotel conference room overlooked Sydney Harbor. Dawn light painted the water copper-gold, same color as Kuro's eyes in the window's reflection. He stood with hands clasped behind his back, studying the unfamiliar geography while Latvala arranged tablets and timing screens on the table with practiced efficiency.

"Close the door, Akira."

The click of the latch changed the room's pressure. No longer casual arrival briefing. Something else.

Latvala pulled up Poland telemetry. SS14 Mikołajki displayed in painful detail—speed traces, throttle application percentages, brake point measurements. The moment before the fast left crest where Kuro's inputs diverged from optimal. Where caution replaced commitment.

"Explain this stage to me."

Kuro watched the data scroll. "Second fastest time. Sufficient to maintain championship lead."

"Evans was faster. Thierry was faster. Sami was faster before his alternator failure." Latvala zoomed on the crest section. "You lifted here. Point-two-eight seconds. Then here." Another zoom. "Point-three-one seconds. And here." Final section. "Point-four-two seconds. Total time lost: one-point-zero-one seconds to optimal pace."

"Within acceptable margin for championship protection."

"That is not what I asked." Latvala's tone stayed neutral. Finnish precision matching Japanese restraint. "I asked you to explain the stage. Not justify the mathematics."

Through the window, Sydney woke. Ferries crossed the harbor, following routes unchanged by championship concerns. Kuro's reflection stared back from the glass—copper eyes dim with exhaustion that had nothing to do with the flight.

"The crest at kilometer seven-point-three was maybe flat," he said. "Recce notes indicated compression depth variable depending on approach angle. Risk assessment determined ninety percent throttle provided adequate speed while eliminating possibility of suspension compression causing loss of control."

"And the second lift point?"

"Left-hand sweep through kilometer nine-point-six. Outside line showed gravel degradation during recce. Inside line required precise entry speed. Margin for error was—"

"—acceptable for stage win," Latvala interrupted. "Evans took inside line at full commitment. Gained three-tenths. No drama."

"Evans is not driving for championship."

The words landed heavy. Truth stated plainly, without decoration or apology.

Latvala studied him. Twenty seconds of silence that felt like evaluation and verdict combined.

"No," he finally said. "Evans is driving to win rallies. You are driving to not lose championships." He pulled up different telemetry. Monte-Carlo. Sweden. Portugal before the title fight calcified into arithmetic. "This is you in January. Flat through compressions. Committed through uncertainties. Fast enough to challenge for rally wins."

"That was when the championship required attack."

"And now it requires what? Survival?" Latvala closed the tablet. "Kuro, you have twenty-five point lead with two rallies remaining. You could finish fifth in Australia and still control your destiny in Japan. The mathematics are comfortable."

"Mathematics become uncomfortable quickly."

"So you drive to make them more comfortable. And in making them comfortable, you stop driving like a champion."

The statement hung between them. Not accusation. Observation delivered with the same analytical precision Latvala had used during his own career—seventeen rally wins, one championship, retirement at thirty-four when the hunger vanished and mathematics became more important than mythology.

Kuro's tail wrapped tighter around his waist. "You want me to attack."

"I want you to drive." Latvala stood, moved to the window. Dawn light caught the gray in his fur, the lines around his eyes that came from decades of calculating risk and reward. "Championships are won through consistency. You have proven this. Two titles, never crashed, never made the spectacular mistake that gifts victories to rivals. Toyota's philosophy made mechanical."

"But?"

"But there is no 'but' in this sentence." Latvala's reflection met Kuro's in the glass. "That is the problem. You have optimized championship winning to the point where rally winning becomes secondary. Stage winning becomes unnecessary. And somewhere in that optimization, you have convinced yourself that driving at ninety percent is the same as driving at one hundred percent with better risk management."

"The outcome is identical if the mathematics—"

"The outcome is you sitting in service parks wondering whether you deserve it."

Kuro's copper eyes flared. Involuntary reaction, the first crack in carefully maintained composure.

Latvala saw it. Nodded slightly.

"Akira tells me things," he said. "Not gossip. Observations. How you review telemetry after victories, searching for the moments you could have gone faster. How you watch Evans and Thierry's stage times and calculate what you could have done differently. How you touch the omamori in your helmet before stages like you are asking permission rather than blessing."

"The shrine grants protection."

"You asked for protection. It has given you championships." Latvala turned from the window. "But protection from what? Crashes? Failure? Or from finding out what happens when you drive at the limit and discover whether talent matches ambition?"

The question landed like gravel against glass. Small impact. Spreading cracks.

Kuro's hands tightened behind his back. "You are asking me to risk the championship."

"I am asking you to win it. Not protect it." Latvala pulled up the Australia route map. "Eighteen stages. Fast, flowing gravel. The kind of rally that rewards commitment over calculation. Evans will be fast—he always is on home terrain. Thierry will attack because twenty-five points down requires miracles, not mathematics. And you will do what? Drive at ninety-five percent and hope Sami's car breaks before yours does?"

"That is not—"

"That is exactly what Poland was." Latvala's voice stayed calm. Teacher correcting student, not boss reprimanding employee. "You drove sufficient pace. Collected sufficient points. Made zero mistakes and zero memories. And you will do the same in Australia unless something changes."

Through the window, Sydney had fully wakened. City moving with purposeful chaos, millions of lives unconcerned with rally championships and the mathematics of protection.

Kuro's reflection stared back. The Shadow. Two-time champion. Winner through arithmetic that felt increasingly hollow the more victories accumulated.

"What are you asking me to do?"

"Drive SS1." Latvala pulled up the stage notes. "Newry. Sixteen-point-four kilometers. Fast and flowing. Natural rhythm. The kind of stage where you either commit to the dance or spend sixteen kilometers calculating steps." He met Kuro's eyes. "I am asking you to drive it like you drove Monte-Carlo SS1 in January. Before the championship became something to protect instead of something to chase."

"And if that loses time? If commitment becomes mistake?"

"Then we discuss it in service and adjust." Latvala's expression softened slightly. "But I think we both know you will not make mistakes if you drive instead of calculate. The talent that won two championships does not disappear when you shift from protection to performance. It just gets buried under mathematics that feel safer than trust."

Kuro looked at the stage notes. Flowing gravel. Natural rhythm. The kind of stage that rewarded instinct over analysis, feel over formula.

The kind of stage he used to love before championships taught him that surviving was smarter than transcending.

"One stage," Latvala said. "SS1. Drive it committed. Not reckless—you are incapable of reckless. But committed. See what time appears. See how it feels. Then make the choice about what the rest of Australia looks like."

"And if Thierry gains time?"

"Then he gains time on one stage of eighteen and you still have twenty-five point cushion." Latvala closed the tablets. "The mathematics protect you even when you stop protecting them. That is what comfortable championship leads provide—freedom to drive instead of defend."

Kuro studied the harbor. Watched ferries trace their routes with the geometric precision he'd built his career on. Thought about Poland SS14, the lift before the crest, the three-tenths lost to Evans and the victory it felt nothing like.

Thought about the omamori against his chest, the prayer for protection that had become something closer to permission to be afraid.

His tail unwrapped slightly. Copper eyes caught dawn light, brightening.

"SS1," he said. "Committed pace."

"Not attack pace. Not protection pace. Just... your pace." Latvala moved toward the door. "Recce starts at ten hundred. Learn the road. Find the rhythm. Remember what it feels like to drive for more than mathematics."

He left.

Akira remained, watching Kuro watch the harbor.

"You do not have to prove anything," the co-driver said quietly. "The championships are real."

"That is not the question Latvala asked."

"No. He asked if you still remember how to enjoy them." Akira stood. "Six hours until recce. You should rest."

"Cannot."

"Will not."

Kuro's reflection showed the truth. Won't sleep. Won't stop calculating. Won't release the mathematics that had carried him to two championships and now felt like the cage protecting him from the question he'd avoided since the season began:

What happens when protection becomes prison?

What happens when you optimize winning to the point where winning feels like loss?

The omamori pressed against his chest. Protection granted, victory delivered, championship approaching through arithmetic that should have felt like triumph.

Should have.

Six hours until recce. Until he learned Australian roads and found their rhythm. Until SS1 Newry and the choice between commitment and calculation, between driving to win or driving to not lose.

Twenty-five point lead over Thierry. Sami forty-one back. Two rallies remaining.

The numbers that defined everything.

The numbers that explained nothing.

Kuro pressed his palm against the window. Glass cold against his pad, Sydney spreading below—unfamiliar city on unfamiliar continent where the championship would be won or lost based on choices he hadn't yet made about what victory meant when mathematics conquered mythology.

"Six hours," he said.

Akira nodded. Left him alone with the harbor and the dawn and the weight of questions that followed champions who stopped believing championships required courage.

The Shadow.

Three-time champion.

Maybe.

If he could remember how to drive like someone chasing glory instead of fleeing failure.

If he could trust talent over arithmetic.

If he could answer the question Latvala posed and Akira confirmed and the omamori couldn't resolve:

Do you deserve it if you never risk losing it?

Kuro stood at the window until recce departure, watching Sydney wake, calculating nothing, feeling everything, preparing for the rally that would determine whether championships were won through survival or achieved through something that looked more like faith.

Australia waited.

The mathematics were comfortable.

That was the problem.
