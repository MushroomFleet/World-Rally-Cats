# Page 5: The Final Stage

The helicopter rotors hammered against wind that tasted of salt and victory. Kuro pressed his forehead against the window, watching the service park shrink below—a temporary city already dismantling itself, Hyundai's blue garages half-collapsed, Toyota's silver structures gleaming in late afternoon sun.

Three months. Thirteen countries. One question answered.

His reflection stared back from the glass: copper eyes dimmed by exhaustion, black fur matted with champagne and dust, the white whisker pattern on his left cheek stark against shadows that had deepened since Monte-Carlo. The 2026 CWRC Champion. Again.

Not the way anyone predicted.

"You're doing it again." Akira's voice carried through the headset, calm as always. Fifteen years of partnership had taught him when Kuro's silence shifted from focused to fractured.

"Doing what?"

"Calculating whether you deserved it."

The omamori from Ise Grand Shrine pressed against Kuro's chest pocket, warm through the fabric of his team polo. He'd carried it through every stage, every service break, every moment when the championship hung on decisions measured in tenths of seconds. The amulet had witnessed what the cameras missed: hands trembling during night stages in Japan, breath catching during Safari's brutal rocks, the instant in Rally Finland when he'd chosen consistency over the glory Sami would have seized.

Kuro closed his eyes. Saw Ott Tänak's pale jade gaze across the podium in Rally Japan, the Estonian's slight nod carrying more weight than any speech. Saw Thierry Neuville's howl of frustration in Chile—so close to defending his title, undone by a single mechanical failure. Saw Sami's tears in Australia when a puncture cost him second in the championship, the young Finn's dreams of beating his teammate shattered against the reality of experience.

"The championship was won in Portugal," Kuro said. Not a question. A confession.

Akira adjusted his glasses, the polarized lenses catching light. "SS14. Amarante. When Sami's tire delaminated and you could have attacked for the stage win."

"I protected second place instead."

"You protected the championship."

The helicopter banked over Tokyo Bay. Somewhere below, in the Nagoya prefecture they'd both left as teenagers chasing rally dreams, Kuro's parents would be preparing the modest celebration he'd requested: family only, no media, no corporate obligations. Just tea and quiet recognition that their son had proven something the sport would spend years dissecting.

That precision could defeat passion.

That the Shadow could outlast the fire.

That rally championships weren't always won in moments of spectacular heroics, sometimes they accumulated in the spaces between—the tire pressure checked one more time, the hybrid deployment held for two seconds longer, the corner taken at ninety-eight percent when rivals gambled everything at one hundred and two.

"Ott texted," Akira said, passing his phone forward.

*Good season. You were faster when it mattered. - OT*

Eleven words from the Ice King. Kuro's thumb hovered over the reply field. What could match that economy? Finally: *Consistency is just patience with speed.*

Send.

The phone buzzed immediately. Ott again: *Philosophy. You're spending too much time with the French.*

Kuro's laugh surprised him—sharp and genuine, the sound foreign in his throat. Below, Tokyo spread like a circuit board, lights beginning to flicker in the gathering dusk. His city. His country that had watched him transform from the prodigy who won everything through raw talent to the champion who'd learned that talent without discipline was just noise.

"Malcolm called while you were with media," Akira continued, systematic as always in delivering information. "Wants to know if you're considering his offer."

M-Sport. The privateer team punching above its weight, Malcolm Wilson's offer casual over drinks in Rally Finland: *When you're done being perfect, come teach my wildcats how to be professional.*

"What did you tell him?"

"That you'd already committed to Toyota through 2028."

Two more years. Two more chances to make it four championships, to cement a legacy that would define an era. Or two more years of Sami growing faster, hungrier, less willing to settle for second chair in Toyota's orchestra of precision.

The GR Yaris Rally1 waited in the service park below, engine cold now, the silver livery still spattered with Australian mud. Car number one. The number reserved for defending champions, for drivers who'd earned the right to sweep the road clean for those following. Kuro had hated that number in Monte-Carlo—hated the target it painted, the expectation it carried, the way it transformed every stage into a referendum on whether dominance could be sustained.

Now, watching Tokyo's lights multiply in the darkness, he understood what Ott had discovered seven years ago: defending a championship wasn't about proving you deserved it. It was about proving you could survive what it cost.

His phone buzzed. Sami, finally: *Brother. You drove like a champion. I drove like I wanted to be champion. Next year, I learn the difference.*

Kuro read it three times. Felt something shift in his chest—not quite pride, not quite relief. Recognition, perhaps, that rivalry could sharpen without cutting. That teammates could push each other toward greatness if neither confused speed with wisdom.

He typed: *Next year, you'll be faster. So will I.*

The helicopter descended toward Toyota's private pad, rotors shifting pitch as landing gear deployed. Through the window, Kuro spotted the welcome committee: team engineers in their pressed white shirts, PR staff with cameras ready, Dr. Nakamura standing slightly apart in his customary position of calm observation.

And at the front, holding a ceramic cup that would contain matcha green tea brewed to exactly the temperature Kuro preferred, Jari-Matti Latvala wore the expression of a team principal who'd just secured manufacturers' championship glory.

"Ready?" Akira asked.

Kuro touched the omamori one final time. Thought of the thirteen rallies, the countless stages, the moments when consistency had felt like cowardice and patience had tasted like surrender. Thought of Thierry's passion that had blazed brilliant and burned out in Chile. Of Ott's ice that had cracked in the Australian heat. Of Sami's raw speed that had shattered against the mathematics of championship points.

He'd won by finishing. By protecting leads when instinct screamed to attack. By choosing the line that guaranteed P2 when P1 demanded gambling everything.

The rally world would debate whether the 2026 championship was won through brilliance or merely avoided through caution. Whether Kuro Nakamura was truly great or simply better at surviving than his rivals were at winning.

He'd spend the off-season wondering the same thing.

The helicopter touched down. The rotors began to slow. Through the window, cameras flashed like stars being born.

Kuro unbuckled his harness and stood, vertebrae cracking after hours of travel, tail uncurling from his waist where he'd kept it wrapped throughout the flight. The omamori settled against his heart. The matcha tea waited in Latvala's steady hands. The GR Yaris sat in the service park, ready to be torn down and analyzed and rebuilt for Monte-Carlo 2027.

"The championship was won in Portugal," he said again, but differently now. Not confession. Acceptance.

Akira nodded. Collected their bags. Opened the helicopter door to the chaos of celebration and obligation and the peculiar loneliness that came with being the best.

Kuro stepped into the Tokyo night, copper eyes catching the camera flashes, and somewhere in the spaces between the lights and the noise, he found what he'd been calculating since Australia: the precise distance between deserving victory and achieving it.

It measured exactly three championships.

And counting.
